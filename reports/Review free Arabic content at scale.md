# Build correctness in, then count it

Arabic World can reach "millions" of varied, review-free Fusha scenarios at $0, but only if it stops thinking of checking as reading sentences and starts thinking of it as proving templates. The research found **no open tool that can judge free Arabic text for case endings and agreement reliably**, no free male MSA voice model with a clean licence, and no phone-sized speech recogniser that grades learner Arabic well. Each gap closes the same way: generate every form by rule from a licence-clean lexicon (**Camel Morph MSA, MIT code and CC BY 4.0 data, over 100K lemmas**), label every lemma universal / cultural / sacred before it is ever used, check 100% of output with deterministic validators, and spend scarce human attention only on a statistical sample per template. Because errors in a template system are systematic, sample size depends on the number of templates, not the number of sentences: **299 clean samples show a template's error rate is under 1% at 95% confidence**, whether it yields a thousand lines or a million. Voices should come from consenting male volunteers recording under a CC0 release, with word and phrase audio covering the long tail. Speaking practice should start as "record yourself and compare", fully on the phone, with cloud recognition only as an opt-in. LLMs have a narrow role: drafting storylet frames offline and acting as an advisory judge calibrated against a human gold set, never as a gate and never as the source of diacritics. A prioritised, costed action list closes the report, and every number marked **[unverified]** must be measured in the lab before anyone relies on it.

## Checking Fusha without an expert means proving templates, not sentences

No open tool can read a fully diacritised Arabic sentence and say "correct" with useful reliability. Arabic grammatical error correction models top out near **F0.5 82.63** (an ensemble, on QALB-14; a single AraBART model scores 76.20) ([ArbESC+](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14230v1)). Their training data, QALB, is web comments and learner essays, mostly without diacritics, so case endings (iʿrāb) are outside what they were built for. Mishkal's syntax analyser uses only links between adjacent words and falls back to defaults such as the accusative when it lacks evidence ([Mishkal](https://github.com/linuxscout/mishkal)). Diacritisers are no better as judges. The 2019 benchmark found neural Shakkala at **2.88% DER** against **13.78%** for the best rule-based tool ([Fadel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01965v1)). The newer CATT model beats eleven systems and GPT-4-turbo ([CATT](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03236)), yet still shows about **8.6% DER and 34% WER on its own harder test set [unverified leaderboard figure]**. A "strip the vowels, re-diacritise, compare" check therefore produces many false alarms. When both sides agree, that is weak evidence, because both can share the same mistake.

Generation should flow the other way. The game already plans storylets with typed slots: ITEM, QTY, PLACE and ROLE, each carrying gender, number, case and definiteness. A generator such as CAMeL Tools' `generate(lemma, feats)` produces the diacritised form by rule. The analyser then confirms that one of its analyses carries exactly the intended lemma, part of speech, case, number, gender and definiteness. That round trip is deterministic. It needs no guessing about which reading was meant, because the template already knows ([CAMeL Tools](https://github.com/CAMeL-Lab/camel_tools); [Camel Morph, LREC-COLING 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.240/)). Syntax-level rules sit in the Arabic language pack as code: number–noun polarity for 3–10, feminine-singular adjectives for non-human plurals, shadda after al- on sun letters, no tanwīn on diptotes, and pausal forms for audio. Neither the notes nor the earlier research cite a grammar authority for these rules. A Muslim-authored grammar reference belongs in `docs/SOURCES.md` before they are frozen.

Humans then check a sample per template, not per sentence. With zero errors in *n* random samples, the 95% upper bound on the error rate is about 3/*n*. That is the "rule of three" (Jovanovic and Levy, 1997) ([Statology](https://www.statology.org/a-concise-guide-to-the-statistical-rule-of-three/)), and zero-acceptance plans in industrial quality control use the same logic ([Minitab](https://support.minitab.com/minitab/help-and-how-to/quality-and-process-improvement/acceptance-sampling/supporting-topics/create-an-accept-on-none-cequals0-plan)).

| Target error rate (95% confidence) | Clean samples needed (zero errors allowed) | One error allowed |
|---|---|---|
| under 5% | 59 | about 93 |
| under 1% | 299 | about 474 |
| under 0.5% | 598 | — |
| under 0.1% | 2,995 | — |

*Own calculation from the standard binomial; the "one error allowed" column is an approximation.*

The catch is that 1,000 templates × 59 samples is 59,000 reviews, far beyond any volunteer pool. The way out is to recognise what kind of errors templates make. A wrong rule fails every instance, so machine enumeration of every (template × lexical class) cell finds rule errors without humans. Random human samples are then needed mainly for lexicon errors: one wrong irregular plural, or a mis-vowelled word. So the human unit of work is the lexicon entry and the structural template family, not the scenario. A reviewer pool should run Tatoeba-style. Native speakers adopt items they find "100% natural", with a self-declared native flag ([Tatoeba wiki](https://en.wiki.tatoeba.org/history/show-version/3287)). Tatoeba itself warns that its corpus is not error-free, and its maintainers argue about non-native reviews ([issue 3080](https://www.github.com/Tatoeba/tatoeba2/issues/3080)). The project should formalise this: two independent reviewers per item, a third on disagreement, and planted known-bad items to measure each reviewer's accuracy. Reviewers make mistakes too, which is why the one-error-allowed column exists.

LLM judges have a limited place here. On Arabic, the closest evidence is the Palm dataset. There an LLM judge correlated with human correctness scores at **Pearson 0.76**, while humans agreed with each other at only **ICC 0.67** ([Palm](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.00151)). That was open-ended answer quality, not grammar. In English grammar correction, an 8B judge reached **Cohen's κ about 0.81** with annotators **[unverified, from a search summary]** ([arXiv 2605.07635](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.07635)). No Arabic grammar-judge agreement study with full diacritics exists in what was found. The project's own build-workflow study (item 9) already sets the rule: a judge is advisory, never a gate. It uses a binary rubric, a different model family from the generator, and a small human gold set, and it reports ranges. Hamel Husain names "trust a judge you never validated against humans" as the classic pitfall ([Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)). Eugene Yan recommends chance-corrected agreement (κ) and aiming for judge–human agreement equal to human–human ([Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)). With rare errors, κ misleads, because class imbalance drags it down even when raw agreement is high ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment.md)). A 2026 paper recommends MCC for binary verdicts ([alphaXiv 2606.00093](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.00093)). The useful number for this project is the judge's **false-accept rate on known errors**. If it is not low on the gold set, the judge only sorts the human queue.

## Review-free comes from labelled lemmas, not from a classifier

No published tool labels Arabic text as universal, cultural or sacred. The nearest relatives are religious hate-speech classifiers trained on tweets, with precision and F1 around **0.76–0.77** in the first public dataset ([Albadi et al. via WANLP 2020](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2020.wanlp-1.2.pdf)). Stronger CNN results near 92–93% come only via a press summary **[unverified]** ([Devdiscourse](https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/technology/3317311-ai-system-achieves-93-accuracy-in-detecting-hate-speech-on-social-media)). Zero-shot classification does worse: **68% macro-F1** on Arabic offensive tweets ([arXiv 2507.23661](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23661v1)). None of these transfers to short game sentences. None is good enough to be the only thing standing between a player and a sacred-content mistake.

The design that works is labelling by construction. Every lemma in the whitelist carries a label. A sentence's label is the highest label of its parts (sacred > cultural > universal), and a scenario's label is the highest label of its sentences. Any unknown, out-of-lexicon word fails the build. The checker is therefore exact, not statistical, and "review-free" becomes a property you can prove for every generated scenario. Classifiers become a second net tuned for recall, because when flagging for human review a missed item costs far more than a false alarm ([arXiv 2207.00111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.00111)). A second hard check catches Quran wording outside the `sanctuary` scenes: a fuzzy match of all generated Arabic against the Tanzil text already in the project. Open-source verse detectors that do exact, normalised and fuzzy matching exist, for example the MIT-licensed `quran-validator`. Its verse dataset carries separate terms, so the project should match against its own Tanzil copy ([Packagist](https://packagist.org/packages/watheqalshowaiter/quran-validator)). The notes propose failing the build on any match of four or more consecutive words **[threshold is a design suggestion, not sourced]**. The Tanzil licence page could not be read this pass ([Tanzil](https://tanzil.net/docs/Quran_Text_License)), so the "verbatim, credited" rule should be rechecked against the live page.

The safest topic families line up with what the CEFR describes for A1–A2 learners: personal information, shopping, local area and work ([CEFR grid](https://lettres.sorbonne-universite.fr/sites/default/files/media/2024-09/CEFRL%20-%20Self-assessment%20grid.pdf)). These also match the project's own "universal first" list:

- numbers and prices
- water, weather and sky
- food and drink
- home and objects
- clock times inside 08:00–11:00
- directions in the quarter
- market errands
- crafts
- greetings and hospitality
- travel

Inside the "neutral" topics, the hard rules become feature-level and lexicon-level bans:

- **Food:** no pork, alcohol or gelatin words, even in "don't eat" form.
- **Money:** no loans, interest, lottery or insurance. Payment is cash and immediate.
- **Music:** no music words at all.
- **Time:** no night, evening, sunset, moon or dinner words.
- **Family:** male kin only. Textbook "family" units normally include mothers and sisters, so this is a deliberate departure.
- **Characters:** human slots can never take feminine gender. That ban is stronger than any word list.

The notes found **no scholarly source** for any of these rulings. They belong on `docs/SCHOLAR_REVIEW.md` as one short list for a single sign-off, not as per-line review.

On measurement, the project already enforces ≥80% universal and ≤12% religious share (now 7.8%). Two refinements matter at scale. First, weight shares by how often each line is actually shown, because a sacred line in every scene matters more than one in a rare scene. Second, add a per-scenario target: at least 90% of generated scenarios universal-only **[proposed target, not sourced]**. On honest wording, faith-oriented children's apps commonly claim "approved by scholars" without naming anyone or describing a process ([Miraj](https://apps.apple.com/US/app/id1222528421); [Rawa](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=net.rawaapp)). No certification scheme was found. Arabic World's published rule set plus a short, complete sacred-content list is already more transparent than that. Until a named scholar signs off, the honest player-facing line is "built on a published rule set; the sacred parts are listed for scholar review", not "scholar approved".

## A licence-clean data stack exists, if three popular resources stay out

The most important licence finding corrects the earlier research, which still had it open. The **Camel Morph repository states that its code is MIT and its MSA database is CC BY 4.0**. App builders are asked to ship the credit "Camel Morph MSA v1.0 © 2024 by New York University Abu Dhabi" ([camel_morph](https://github.com/CAMeL-Lab/camel_morph)). That makes it the right source for every diacritised paradigm the game ships. The older CAMeL Tools data packages are different: `morphology-db-msa-r13` (40.5 MB) and `disambig-mle-calima-msa-r13` are **GPL v2** ([CAMeL packages](https://camel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/packages.html)). The two notes disagree on whether using r13 only at build time avoids GPL obligations. The open-data note argues that shipped tables derived from it are derivative works. The safe reading is to generate nothing that ships from r13, and to use it at most as a yes/no cross-check (a licence inference, not legal advice). The repo also lists some Camel Morph specification spreadsheets as "only upon demand". The shipped `.db` file is enough for generation, so no request is needed unless the project wants to extend the grammar.

The rest of the stack sorts cleanly:

## Free male voices: no open model ships clean, so record people

None of the TTS options found is free to ship, documented as male, and built for diacritised MSA all at once:

- **Meta's MMS-TTS Arabic** is CC BY-NC 4.0 ([HF](https://huggingface.co/facebook/mms-tts-ara)).
- **Coqui XTTS-v2** is non-commercial for both weights and generated audio, with no commercial route since Coqui closed ([XTTS-v2](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v2/blob/v2.0.1/README.md?code=true)).
- **Piper's only Arabic voice, "kareem"**, is labelled MIT and is about 127 MB for both sizes ([piper-voices](https://huggingface.co/rhasspy/piper-voices/tree/main/ar/ar_JO)). But it is tagged Jordanian, its training-data repo has no licence file ([arabicttstrain](https://github.com/AliMokhammad/arabicttstrain)), and a reseller calls it non-commercial ([tts.ai](https://tts.ai/voices/piper-kareem-arabic/)). Its gender is also unconfirmed. That makes it unresolved, not safe.
- **Edge "Read Aloud" (edge-tts)** is an unofficial endpoint, and using it may breach Microsoft's terms ([MS Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/2088770/are-opensource-edge-tts-free-for-commercial-use)).
- **Azure**: the project's existing decision to ship only paid-tier Azure renders stands, because free-tier output rights are disputed even in Microsoft's own Q&A ([MS Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5805156/please-clarify-the-conflicting-information-regardi)).

The data side is no cleaner. The Arabic Speech Corpus (single male, Damascene accent) is listed as CC BY on its own site and CC BY-NC-SA elsewhere ([ASC](https://en.arabicspeechcorpus.com/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_Speech_Corpus)). ArVoice is research-only ([arXiv 2505.20506](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20506v1)). ClArTTS is one male speaker reading Classical Arabic ([arXiv 2303.00069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00069)). Common Voice Arabic is CC0 and large (**158 h recorded, 92 h validated, 1,654 speakers**) but crowd-quality and mixed gender ([Mozilla Data Collective](https://datacollective.mozillafoundation.org/datasets/cmflnuzw47jng4x81tavetpul)), so it suits speech-recognition tests better than voices.

The one promising model is **SILMA TTS**. It has Apache-2.0 weights **[confirm on the official repo; mirrors differ]**, 150M parameters and explicit full-tashkeel support. It clones a voice from under 8 seconds of reference audio and requires consent for any cloned voice ([HF silma-tts](https://huggingface.co/harfby/silma-tts)). That points to one clean route. Recruit a few male native-speaker volunteers on the LibriVox model: a cheap USB mic, Audacity, no audition, a public-domain dedication and proof-listening against the script ([LibriVox](https://wiki.librivox.org/index.php/Newbie_Guide_to_Recording)). Validate each clip with two listeners, as Common Voice does ([Common Voice](https://pontoon.mozilla.org/fub/common-voice/web/locales/common-voice/en/pages/about.ftl)). Each volunteer signs a short CC0 release that names game use, AI cloning and withdrawal. Their recordings then cover the core lines directly and serve as SILMA's consented reference voices for the long tail. SILMA runs at about **0.12× real time on an RTX 4090**, so rendering on Edward's Mac or a free cloud GPU is plausible but **[unmeasured]**.

Scale changes the audio plan. At about 16 kbps Opus, **one million three-second lines is about 6 GB [unverified, own arithmetic]**. That is too much for free static hosting. Audio should therefore cover a curated core of tens of thousands of lines, plus per-lemma and per-phrase clips for generated scenarios, with text always available. Stitched clips lose natural prosody. That is acceptable for word-tap audio but not for full NPC lines, which should come from the curated set. Every voice must pass a native listener's test on case-ending minimal pairs (-u / -a / -i), because no published quality score covers any of these voices on diacritised MSA.

## Speaking practice starts with record-and-compare on the phone

Browser speech recognition exists on both phone platforms: partial support in iOS Safari from 14.5, partial in Chrome Android ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/speech-recognition)). By default it sends audio to Apple's or Google's servers. On-device mode (`processLocally` plus a downloaded language pack) is new, and nothing confirms that it works for Arabic on phones ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition)). Chromium may ask the user's permission to install a pack in a language other than the phone's main one ([blink-dev](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/VNOok2dbmHM)). Whether it works in installed iOS home-screen web apps is contested ([whatpwacando.today](https://whatpwacando.today/speech-recognition); [WebKit 239816](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=239816)). The `grammars` feature that once restricted answers to a list is deprecated and does nothing. Constrained checking therefore has to happen in the game's own code: take the top alternatives, strip diacritics, normalise alef, yā' and tā' marbūṭa, and accept any alternative within a small edit distance of the 1–5 expected answers.

On-device models are heavy and weak on Arabic:

- **Whisper tiny/base** in the browser download at roughly **120 MB and 210 MB** ([offlinetts.com](https://offlinetts.com/blog/browser-speech-recognition-whisper-comparison/)). The model card warns of hallucination and weak accuracy on low-resource accents ([whisper-tiny](https://huggingface.co/openai/whisper-tiny)).
- **Safari pages** are killed under memory pressure ([WebKit 311598](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=311598)).
- **The Apache-2.0 Arabic wav2vec2 model** reports **39.6% WER** on Common Voice ([model card](https://huggingface.co/jonatasgrosman/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53-arabic)).
- **Vosk's MSA model** is 318 MB ([Vosk](https://alphacephei.com/vosk/models)).
- **MMS-1b** is non-commercial ([HF](https://huggingface.co/facebook/mms-1b-all)).

Phoneme-level pronunciation scoring for MSA exists in research (IqraEval). It uses 300M–1B-parameter models and data centred on Quran recitation ([IqraEval.2](https://iqraeval-iqraeval-interspeech-26.static.hf.space/index.html)), which clashes with keeping Quran material out of everyday scenes.

That fixes the order of tiers:

- **Tier 0 (default):** the player hears the volunteer's line, records himself with `MediaRecorder`, plays both back and rates himself. No audio leaves the phone. It works everywhere and costs nothing.
- **Tier 1:** on-device Web Speech, only where `SpeechRecognition.available()` reports a ready local Arabic pack.
- **Tier 2:** cloud Web Speech, only after a one-sentence consent screen saying the voice goes to Apple or Google.
- **Tier 3:** an optional Wi-Fi download of Whisper, used only for constrained matching.

Speaking stays optional, and nothing in progress depends on it. All of this is design reasoning **untested on hardware**. The project's planned Suite S needs one iPhone and one Android spike before any tier above 0 ships.

## Millions of scenarios need state axes, a bounded pipeline and locked checks

Raw combinatorics is the wrong goal. Kate Compton's "10,000 bowls of oatmeal" is the warning: technically distinct outputs that players can't tell apart ([CMU reading](https://ems.andrew.cmu.edu/2021s/60210a/minniebzrg/02/15/minniebzrg-02reading/index.html)). Rabii and Cook proved a limit that applies directly: how intricate a generator's output can be is bounded by the knowledge put into the generator ([FDG 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02131)). For a learner, synonym shuffling is worse than oatmeal, because each swap is a new unknown word. Meaningful variety comes from authored state axes that change the learner's task: errand, missing item, weather, time inside the window, who is present, and complication (out of stock, wrong change). Selection then picks the most specific storylet for the current state, as in Valve's response rules ([Valve wiki](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Response_System)) and Emily Short's salience-based narrative ([Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)). Variety should be reported as **distinct (axis-tuple × learning target) situations**, not distinct strings. A constraint solver can enumerate coherent outlines offline: Wang and Kreminski produced **401,990 distinct outlines** with clingo ([arXiv 2406.00554](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00554)). The game ships verified tables, and the phone only looks them up. Difficulty uses known-lemma coverage, verified at build time in the way SRS-Stories checks LLM stories against a learner's word list ([arXiv 2512.18362](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.18362v1)). Coverage targets 95–98% ([Re-RIGHT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05302v2)), counted on lemmas because Arabic clitics would inflate surface forms. No Arabic-specific threshold was found.

Coverage of that space can be proved cheaply. NIST's fault studies found most interaction failures involve one or two factors, and none were reported beyond six ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/1637006)). One study found **96% of faults were single or two-way** for one application type ([Kuhn slides](https://csrc.nist.gov/CSRC/media/Projects/cyber-supply-chain-risk-management/documents/SSCA/Fall_2019/WedAM2.2_kuhn.pdf)). But random testing matched three-way coverage in another study ([Kuhn et al. 2009](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130217091851/http:/csrc.nist.gov/groups/SNS/acts/documents/kuhn-kacker-lei-hunter09.pdf)). The lab should therefore report a script-computed **2-way coverage over the valid axis space (3-way where axes interact), plus a random-seed sample**. Combinations ruled out by design should be listed as invalid, not silently missing.

The project's build-workflow study supplies the reliability frame, and five of its items apply directly:

- **Item 14, bounded pipeline:** generation runs as schema → deterministic realisation → Suite R and C rule functions, with a hard step cap. Code, never a model, produces Arabic forms.
- **Item 12, mutation self-test:** every validator must reject planted bad items before a cycle counts. Plant a feminine human slot, a 14:00 clock, an off-list lemma, a broken number agreement, a four-word Quran match outside the sanctuary, and a music word.
- **Item 8, held-out tests:** keep a held-out gold set and held-out play-throughs that the generator and tuner never see. When suites reach 100%, add harder cases instead of celebrating.
- **Item 2, locked rules:** the hard-rule suites are locked by hash and a pre-commit hook, so an improve step can never loosen a check to pass.
- **Item 1, evidence-only reports:** every count in a cycle report comes from logged output, and a missing result is "unknown", never "pass".

Duolingo's "shadow mode" fits the same discipline: log what a new selector would have chosen before switching it on ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain)). The open-weight Arabic LLMs noted in earlier research, Fanar and Jais (Apache 2.0), stay in their narrow role. They draft storylet frames offline, which are then stripped back to slots and rebuilt through the deterministic path.

## Conclusion

The research turns a scale problem into a counting problem. Correctness, review-freeness and licence cleanliness all become properties of a few hundred templates and a few thousand lemma entries, not of millions of lines. The real constraint is the number of templates and lexicon entries a small volunteer pool can certify, so authoring effort, not sentence count, is what grows or limits the game. Adding a template has a known human price (about 59–299 clean samples, depending on the confidence wanted), and adding generated scenarios inside certified templates costs nothing.

The weakest links are human, not technical: male native-speaker volunteers for voices and review, and one scholar sign-off on a short list of rulings. Both are public, reversible-only-with-effort decisions that only Edward can make. Everything else on the list is lab work agents can do at $0. Each claimed number should stay marked until the lab or a real phone confirms it.
