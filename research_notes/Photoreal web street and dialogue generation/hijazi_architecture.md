## 1. Measured dimensions: heights, storeys, walls, floors

### Takeaway
Tower houses were typically 5–6 storeys (the richest up to 7), built of coral/mangabi stone with load-bearing walls 50–60 cm thick at the bottom, thinning upward, tied every ~1–1.2 m of height by horizontal imported timber courses (gandal) that show on both faces of the wall. Floor-to-floor heights were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Main structure: "5-6 storey traditional buildings in old Jeddah", made possible by coral stone plus timber — [M] [Nyazi, "The Traditional Coral Stone Buildings of the Red Sea Coast: Case Study of Historic Jeddah", Gazi Univ. J. Sci. Part B 6(4):159-165, 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Wealthy merchants built "elaborate, seven storey tower houses" with oriel windows shielded by roshans (UNESCO nomination text, as quoted in search snippet; page itself 403 in this session) — [N] [UNESCO WHC 1361 document](https://whc.unesco.org/document/152474)
- UNESCO describes the fabric as "isolated tower houses, lower coral stone houses, mosques, ribat-s, souks and small public squares" (snippet) — [N] [UNESCO WHC 1361](https://whc.unesco.org/document/152474)
- Houses were "narrow and multistory ... to catch the breeze and to fit in the limited space within the old city wall" — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Load-bearing walls "approximately 50-60 centimeters thick, starting thicker then reduced in thickness on upper floors" — [M] [Nyazi 2018, citing Al-Lyaly 1990](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Horizontal gandal timber courses reinforce coral walls "at every 1.20 m. or so" (citing King 1989); elsewhere in the same paper "leaving a one meter gap between each one"; the reinforcing members "were visible on both external and internal faces of walls"; the topmost tier ties into the floor joists — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Foundations: ~1 m deep excavation, footings of coral stone and clay — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Floor build-up: timber boards on timber joists "about 15 centimeters in diameter" (i.e. round poles), straw mats, 4 cm wet earth, 3 cm dry earth, 3 cm pebble-lime mix, floor tiles — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Roof: same as floor plus a thick limestone bed and water-resistant lime plaster on top; 7 cm pressed dry earth layer for insulation — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Staircase wound around a central pier enclosed by thick walls (a "massive hollow column"); coral-block steps with a 5 x 5 cm timber nosing — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Openings use timber lintels of "stout parallel timbers across the thickness of the wall" or masonry arches — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Second source on walls: Baeshen House (Jeddah, built 1856) has Al-Mangabi stone walls of "70–80 cm thickness"; rooms laid along north and west axes with large bay windows on the west and north façades to catch cool breezes; the south side closed against hot southern winds; upper floors airier than lower — [N? author Saudi-named, institution US] [Basunbul & Braham, Univ. of Pennsylvania, Frontiers in Built Environment 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2023.1127615/full). Conflict: Nyazi gives 50–60 cm; use 50–80 cm at ground floor as the sourced range.
- Uses by floor: ground-floor reception, first-floor guest lodging, upper floors for the family — [M] [Omrania (Riyadh architects), "8 Features of the Vernacular Architecture of the Hejaz"](https://omrania.com/insights/8-features-vernacular-architecture-hejaz)

### Inferences
- With timber bands every ~1–1.2 m, a 3.5–4 m storey would show about 3 visible dark horizontal bands per floor on unplastered or worn walls — a strong, cheap texture cue (horizontal dark stripes in the plaster/stone).
- Modelling defaults (inferred, not measured): 5 storeys for "big" houses, 2–3 for "lower coral stone houses"; thick walls should read as deep window reveals (≈0.5 m on the ground floor, less above).

### Gaps
- Floor-to-floor heights and total building heights in metres: not found (expected in Al-Lyaly 1990 thesis ch. 2–3 and the UNESCO dossier; both unreadable this session). UNVERIFIED common figure: ground floor ~4 m, upper floors ~3.5 m — do not treat as sourced.
- Plot sizes / footprint dimensions: not found.

## 2. Materials and colours

### Takeaway
Off-white lime-washed render over coral/mangabi stone is the dominant surface; pastel yellow, cream, blue and pink washes and ochre plaster also occur. Woodwork is imported teak ("Jawi"), with mahogany and sisam; roshans are natural brown wood or painted green (blue/white painted roshans not confirmed by a source here).

### Cited Findings
- Mangabi ("Hajar al-manqabi", after Al-Manqabah lagoon) is a coquina (shell) limestone, easy to cut when fresh, hardens in air, porous, light, insulating; coral blocks were also used, often mixed with limestone masonry — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Mortar: dark-brown clay from the Al-Manqabah lagoon bed mixed with lime — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Timber: hard timber from Africa and Indonesia for beams; "gandal" from India for wall reinforcement and floors; "Jawi" (teak, from Java) for front doors, roshans and windows; also mahogany and sisam for doors/windows — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Roshan woods also listed as teak, Javan, mahogany and other African/Middle-Eastern woods — [M] [Adas, King Abdulaziz Univ., ISPRS Archives XL-5/W2, 2013](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XL-5-W2/7/2013/)
- "Houses were usually whitewashed or painted in pastel shades of yellow, cream, blue and pink" — [M] [Nyazi 2018, citing Kamal 2014 and Bagader 2016](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Lime plaster finished with lime wash; "thick coat of white or ochre plaster" protects the stone; white lime wash "reflects 80% or more of solar radiation" — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Roshan colours: "wooden, brown shades, and green" — [M] [Saudipedia, "Al-Rawashin"](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)
- Coral stone, silt clay and palm wood give the architecture its characteristic colours and textures — [M] [Omrania](https://omrania.com/insights/8-features-vernacular-architecture-hejaz)

### Inferences
- Palette for the game: base walls warm off-white / cream lime; a minority of houses in pale ochre-yellow, pale blue or pale pink wash; wood in mid/dark teak brown, some roshans in faded green. Stone exposed where render has fallen (rough, pale grey-beige, shelly, porous).
- Lime wash is matte and chalky; weathered surfaces should be blotchy, not glossy.

### Gaps
- Blue- or white-painted roshans: commonly seen in photos but not confirmed by a text source this session (UNVERIFIED).
- No measured mangabi block sizes found.

## 3. Roshan (rawasheen) anatomy, types and lattice

### Takeaway
Roshans are projecting teak bays carried on wooden brackets (kabbush/kabaish), in three broad types: the flat "masmat" (slight projection, plain), the projecting "bariz" (rich geometric lattice, often with a crown/canopy), and balcony-type units. They may be single, stacked floor-on-floor, or run continuously across a floor; in Makkah they formed vertical ranks or wrapped whole facades. A Spanish-language Jeddah study gives a typical width of 2.4 m; projection, height and lattice dimensions were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: projecting windows/balconies of wooden panels covering a house's exterior openings, in Jeddah, Makkah, Madinah, Yanbu and Taif; recorded from the 12th century — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)
- Types: (1) Al-Masmat — simplest, slight projection, little carving, usually no crown, runs roof-to-ground near the entrance, rectangular or arched windows with carved iron bars; (2) Al-Bariz — intricate geometric pattern, usually above entrances, continuous across a floor or stacked units each with its own design, often a crown or hollow canopy on top; (3) Roshan with balcony — separate units forming a projecting balcony, with an outward or flat shade/canopy — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)
- Supported by wooden cantilevers; "small lattice screens are common" — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)
- Functions: let in and regulate light through movable panels, ventilation, view out with privacy, opening fully or partly to control view, glare and air; sometimes extends a room over the street — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin); [M] [Adas 2013](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XL-5-W2/7/2013/)
- Makkah rawashin described as the tallest and largest of their kind, in stacked rows from ground upward or wrapping horizontally around upper floors to cover the whole facade — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)
- Makkah roshan read in three parts — upper ("forehead"), middle, lower — each with its own ornament and function (i.e. cap / body / base) — [M] [Al-Murahhem, "The Mechanism of the Rawāshīn: The Case Study of Makkah", WIT Trans. Ecol. Env. 128](https://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-ecology-and-the-environment/128/20814). (Note: the paper's anthropomorphic metaphor must NOT be modelled literally — hard rule: no faces.)
- Most historic Makkah rawasheen buildings were demolished for the expansion around the Haram — [M] [Al-Murahhem](https://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-ecology-and-the-environment/128/20814)
- Roshan "normally measures 2.4 m of width" (search snippet of a Spanish-language Jeddah roshan study; full text not read) — [riunet.upv.es / UPC, via search](https://riunet.upv.es/bitstream/10251/76207/1/2316-9659-1-PB.pdf) — treat as PROVISIONAL
- Parts named in Arabic coverage: the projecting "ghoula" part, "shish" (louvred shutters) and stone brackets (kawabil) carrying the roshan (search snippet of Al Arabiya, 2024; article timed out) — [M] [Al Arabiya](https://www.alarabiya.net/saudi-today/2024/04/21/-الرواشين-الحجازية-تاريخ-معماري-عتيق-والجهات-المعنية-تحافظ-عليه) — PROVISIONAL
- An Egyptian academic study (AmeSea journal, 2021) says the roshan rests on wooden supports ("kabbush") extended from the room floor; a snippet mentions a 32 cm projection limit but it is unclear whether it refers to the mashrabiya or the roshan — [M] [AmeSea](https://amesea.journals.ekb.eg/article_195992_63162ae114980412d64c5258bcaa137c.pdf) — UNVERIFIED number
- Roshan/mashrabiya concept influenced by Egypt via pilgrims and craftsmen — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Decay: moisture damage, bad repairs and joinery, unsuitable cleaners and paints, mismatched replacement woods — [M] [Adas 2013](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XL-5-W2/7/2013/)
- Dakka: stone or brick bench outside the house, height no more than 60 cm, running the width of the wall, under the rawashin — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)

### Inferences
- Modelling kit: base (corbelled brackets + bottom apron panel), body (vertical rhythm of panels: lower solid/carved panels, middle lattice or louvred shish panels that hinge/slide, upper fixed lattice), cap (cornice + optional crown/canopy). Width ~2.4 m per unit (provisional); 1–3 units per facade per floor for a typical house, stacked vertically over the entrance, or a continuous band on a grand house.
- Lattice: two looks are needed — flat geometric lattice (sawn strips, cheap as an alpha texture) and louvred shutters (horizontal slats). Turned-wood (Cairo-style "mashrabiya" bead) grilles are less characteristic of Hijazi roshans per the "roshan vs mashrabiya" distinction, but this was not confirmed from a primary source.
- Projection (inferred from photos, UNVERIFIED): roughly 0.5–0.9 m; treat only as an art estimate.

### Gaps
- Roshan projection depth, height, lattice bar size and pattern geometry: not verified (Al-Lyaly thesis ch. on "the rawshan" and the AmeSea paper are the right sources; both unreadable this session).
- Number per facade: no source gave a count.

## 4. Doors, windows, roofs and rooftop elements

### Takeaway
Front doors were teak, carved, and status symbols; ground-floor windows of the "masmat" kind have carved iron bars; roofs were flat lime-plastered terraces with parapets, used for sleeping, with a light timber top-floor room ("mabit") of louvred panels. No door or parapet dimensions were found.

### Cited Findings
- Front doors, roshans and windows made of teak ("Jawi"); also mahogany and sisam — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Doors welcomed guests and signalled the owner's character and status; geometric patterns, calligraphy and plant forms around doors and windows — [M] [Omrania](https://omrania.com/insights/8-features-vernacular-architecture-hejaz)
- Al-mabit: top-floor room of panelled woodwork with movable louvres and a light roof, warm by day, cool at night, used for sleeping — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Roof terraces used as living space; families slept there in summer — [M] [Omrania](https://omrania.com/insights/8-features-vernacular-architecture-hejaz)
- Al-Lyaly's thesis has sections on "parapet walls around house terraces", "al-mabit and kharjat" and "house–street relationship" (headings only seen) — [M?] [Al-Lyaly 1990, Univ. of Edinburgh PhD (Saudi author; also in Saudi Digital Library)](https://drepo.sdl.edu.sa/items/000c8d02-7013-4e79-9c01-c82856f0f598/full)
- City houses had chimneys/projections above the roof parapet (western-region styles list) — [M] [Saudipedia, Traditional Architectural Styles in the Western Region](https://saudipedia.com/en/list-of-the-traditional-architectural-styles-in-the-western-region)
- Modern damage on roofs: added floors, cement screeds, heavy water tanks and metal structures on roofs — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)

### Inferences
- Rooftop props for a lived-in feel: parapet, a small louvred timber mabit, water tanks (modern, plain), satellite-free if a "timeless" look is wanted.
- Door: double-leaf teak in a stone/plaster frame with carved lintel panel; deep reveal from the thick wall.

### Gaps
- Door width/height, parapet height and pattern (pierced vs solid), window sizes and rhythm: not found.

## 5. Streets, squares, souqs and climate

### Takeaway
Lanes were narrow and often dead-end, giving semi-private residential alleys; tall narrow houses and canopies maximised shade on the walking routes; houses are tall to catch breeze. No measured widths were verified this session.

### Cited Findings
- Smaller alleys serving residential quarters were semi-public; through-passage prevented by a dead-end network — [M?] [Sustainable-oriented development for the urban interface of Historic Jeddah (typeset)](https://typeset.io/pdf/sustainable-oriented-development-for-urban-interface-of-ldgf6fft.pdf) (snippet)
- Narrow alleys and close buildings plus shaded canopies maximise the shade cast on pedestrian routes during the day — [M] [Ajel (Saudi news)](https://english.ajel.sa/saudi-arabia/bgfmfsh8i) (snippet)
- Street width studied as a variable "which allows neighbors contacts without visual connection" — [WIT Press IHA20 paper](https://witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/IHA20/IHA20013FU1.pdf) (snippet)
- Historic Jeddah named quarters include Al-Sham, Al-Mazloum, Al-Bahr and Al-Yemen — [M] [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/al-rawashin)
- Fabric includes souks and "small public squares" (UNESCO) — [N] [UNESCO 1361](https://whc.unesco.org/document/152474)
- Climate: white lime wash reflects solar radiation; thick walls delay heat; roshans and tall forms catch breeze — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547)
- Orientation: big bay windows (roshans) on north and west facades to catch prevailing breezes; south side kept closed — [Basunbul & Braham 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2023.1127615/full)
- Lighting study (Waheeb 2023, via search snippet): the roshan is a medium-size daylight source, the "kharja" (a larger projecting element) the large source — [MDPI/related search result](https://mdpi.com/1996-1073/17/9/1995) — PROVISIONAL

### Inferences
- For 08:00–11:00 game lighting: with narrow lanes and 4–6-storey walls, the sun (low in the east early, rising) lights only the upper facades and roshans of west-side walls while the lane floor stays in shade; small squares (baraha) are the bright pools of light. This fits the art-bible "raking morning sun".
- Modelling estimate (UNVERIFIED): residential lanes ~2–4 m, souq streets wider with awnings/canopies; H:W well above 2:1. Use as art guidance only.

### Gaps
- Lane, souq street and baraha dimensions; orientation data: not verified (UPC and MDPI studies unreadable).
- Morning activities/objects: no source read this session; rely on general observation and keep neutral (shop shutters opening, sweeping, carts, water delivery, bread, tea), all-male per project hard rule.

## 6. Famous buildings that must NOT be copied

### Takeaway
Avoid replicating any named landmark. Confirmed named houses in sources: Bayt Nasif (ArchNet site 3794) and Bayt Hazazi (Jeddah Historic District / Ministry of Culture). Bayt Matbouli and Bayt Noorwali are also well-known landmarks (not confirmed by a source this session). Also do not model any real mosque of Al-Balad or Masjid an-Nabawi.

### Cited Findings
- Bayt Nasif (Nassif) house, pictured as the example of al-mabit — [M] [Nyazi 2018](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/624547); [M] [ArchNet site 3794](https://archnet.org/sites/3794)
- Bayt Hazazi and surrounding buildings, documented by Jeddah Historic District / Ministry of Culture — [M] [Google Arts & Culture story](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/IgVhUr6c8o8tHQ)

### Inferences
- Originality strategy: compose houses from the kit (stone/plaster box + timber bands + roshan modules + door + parapet), vary roshan count, colour and stacking per house; never reproduce a known landmark's massing (e.g. Nasif's large corner tower with a tree in front) or its exact roshan layout.

### Gaps
- Bayt Matbouli, Bayt Noorwali, Bayt Al-Sharbatly, Bayt Al-Jamjoom, and the Al-Shafi'i and Al-Mi'mar mosques are commonly cited landmarks (UNVERIFIED in this session) — treat all as "do not copy".
